From Promises to Proof: Making Age Signals Enforceable

Recent industry settlements and global experiments show a hard truth: age verification inside apps is brittle and easily bypassed. Connecticut can require a different approach — one that preserves privacy, closes the accountability gap, and forces platforms to deliver real child protections.

Why Age Verification Alone Fails

Public reporting on recent platform settlements highlights a central problem: age verification depends on methods that are easy to bypass. When Australia banned social media for under‑16s in 2025, teens found workarounds within days. Platforms promise better verification, but the technical and behavioral realities make app‑level age checks unreliable.

Age verification inside apps tries to guess who the user is. That approach has repeatedly failed in practice.

Parents Already Verify the Device — Privacy Preserved

Parents verify their child when they create a child account using Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link. That verification happens at the operating‑system level — not inside third‑party apps.

The Child Mode API does not share personal data with apps. It sends one non‑identifying safety flag:

child = true

Apps do not receive name, birthday, contacts, precise location, photos, or behavioral data. They receive a yes/no safety signal that allows them to activate the protections they already have.

The Liability Loophole and Why It Matters

Today, platforms rely on self‑reported age. If a child lies, platforms can say they had no reason to treat the user as a minor. That creates a structural incentive to avoid reliable age signals.

A universal Child Mode API changes the incentives: once the OS sends a verified child flag, platforms must activate youth protections and can no longer hide behind “the child lied.” Removing that loophole is the single most effective way to force companies to build real, enforceable protections.

In short: age verification protects platforms; age signaling protects children.

Policy Ask

Require OS‑Level Age Signaling and App Enforcement

We ask Connecticut lawmakers to require: OS‑level Age Signaling (a verified child flag), mandatory Child Mode API implementation by apps, app‑store enforcement, transparency about default youth settings, and country‑level origin for public accounts. This is privacy‑preserving and technically feasible.

Implementation · Enforcement · Privacy

How Age Signaling Works — Practical, Private, Enforceable

Age Signaling is not a theory — it is a simple, privacy‑preserving connection between the parent‑verified device and the protections apps already have. Below is how it works in practice, what apps must do, and how enforcement and liability change the incentives for platforms.

How It Works — The Simple Flow

1) A parent creates a child account using Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link and verifies the child at the OS level.
2) The operating system exposes a single, non‑identifying safety flag to apps when that child account is active: child = true.
3) Apps receive the flag and automatically activate the protections they already support (child/teen modes, restricted DMs, limited discovery, content filters, spending limits, parental notifications).

This flow verifies the device/account relationship — not the child’s identity — and it requires no new personal data to be shared with third‑party apps.

Privacy Guarantee — What Is and Is Not Shared

The Child Mode API sends one thing only: a yes/no safety signal. It does not share any personally identifying information with apps.

  • Not shared: name; birthday; contacts; photos; precise location; browsing history; behavioral data.
  • Shared: a single non‑identifying flag: child = true.

The operating system already holds the parent‑verified information. The API simply allows apps to honor that verification without receiving any sensitive data.

What Apps Must Do When They Receive the Child Flag

When an app receives child = true, it should automatically:

  • Enable child/teen modes and safe defaults.
  • Disable or restrict direct messaging and friend discovery for minors.
  • Limit algorithmic recommendations and surface content controls.
  • Disable in‑app chat, location sharing, and spending features unless explicitly enabled by a parent.
  • Prevent silent activation of new features for minors.
  • Notify parents when protections are turned off or when high‑risk features are requested.

Most major apps already have these controls; the Child Mode API simply ensures they are used for the youngest users by default.

Liability, Incentives, and Why This Will Force Change

Today, platforms rely on self‑reported age. If a child lies, platforms can say they had no reason to treat the user as a minor. That creates a structural incentive to avoid reliable age signals and to keep defaults that maximize engagement.

A universal Child Mode API removes that loophole. Once the OS sends a verified child flag, platforms can no longer claim “the child lied.” That legal clarity changes incentives: platforms must activate protections or face regulatory and legal scrutiny. In short, removing the liability shield forces companies to build effective protections.

This is the accountability layer the industry has been missing — it aligns legal responsibility with the parent‑verified reality already present on devices.

Why Age Verification Alone Has Failed

Recent reporting on platform settlements has made a key point clear: age verification inside apps is brittle and easily bypassed. For example, when Australia implemented a social media ban for under‑16s in 2025, teens found workarounds quickly. Public statements and settlements (including recent coverage of Meta) emphasize that verification promises are only as good as the enforcement mechanism behind them.

The Child Mode API addresses this exact failure: it does not rely on a user typing an age into an app. It relies on the parent‑verified account at the OS level — a signal apps cannot reasonably dispute.

Age verification tries to guess who the user is. Age signaling knows the device is a child account.

Enforcement, Transparency, and Compliance

We recommend a three‑part enforcement approach:

  • App‑store rules: Require apps to honor the child flag as a condition of listing and updates.
  • Transparency reporting: Require apps to publish default settings for minors and a short, plain‑language explanation of protections activated by the child flag.
  • Regulatory oversight: Allow state enforcement (AG or consumer protection) to audit compliance and require corrective action when defaults are not honored.

These mechanisms are consistent with existing app‑store and consumer‑protection frameworks and are technically straightforward to implement.

Accountability · Metrics · Roadmap

Holding Platforms Accountable — Clear Commitments, Measurable Outcomes

A Child Mode API only protects children if companies use it, regulators enforce it, and families can see the results. This section defines the commitments we expect, the metrics that prove progress, and the enforcement roadmap Connecticut can adopt to make Age Signaling real.

Company Commitments — What We Expect from Apps and Platforms

When the OS exposes child = true, companies should commit to:

  • Honor the child flag immediately and by default for all parent‑managed accounts.
  • Document and publish default youth settings in plain language (communication, discovery, content, spending).
  • Provide a parental control dashboard that shows which protections are active and how to change them.
  • Log and report when protections are turned off and require parental re‑authorization for high‑risk changes.
  • Design product roadmaps that prioritize safety improvements for child accounts and publish timelines for delivery.

These are operational commitments — not aspirational statements. They must be auditable and time‑bound.

Metrics & Reporting — How Progress Is Measured

To demonstrate compliance and progress, platforms should publish quarterly reports with the following metrics:

  • Percentage of active child accounts that received and honored child = true.
  • Number and type of high‑risk features disabled by default for child accounts (DMs, discovery, location, spending).
  • Counts of parental notifications sent and parental re‑authorizations requested/approved.
  • Incidents involving minors escalated to safety teams and outcomes (redacted for privacy).
  • Time to remediate reported failures to honor child defaults.

Reports should be published in machine‑readable and human‑readable formats and made available to regulators and independent auditors.

Audit & Enforcement — Practical Mechanisms

We recommend a layered enforcement model that fits existing consumer‑protection frameworks:

  • App‑store compliance: Require proof of Child Mode API support as part of app review and updates.
  • Regulatory audits: Allow state consumer protection or AG offices to audit compliance, request logs, and require corrective action.
  • Independent audits: Require periodic third‑party audits for large platforms with public summaries of findings.
  • Remedies: Noncompliance should trigger graduated remedies: notices, fines, removal from app stores for repeated violations, and mandatory remediation plans.

Enforcement should focus on outcomes (did the child flag result in protections?) rather than internal implementation details.

Sample Statutory Language (Draft for Staff)

The following is a short, staff‑friendly draft to illustrate how Age Signaling and enforcement could be codified:

Section X. OS‑Level Age Signaling.
Operating system vendors shall provide an API that indicates when a device user is a minor under a parent‑managed account. The API shall expose a non‑identifying flag (e.g., child = true) and shall not transmit personal identifying information to third‑party applications.

Section Y. App Obligations.
Applications receiving the child flag shall automatically enable youth‑appropriate defaults for communication, discovery, content, and spending. Applications shall publish default settings for minors and provide parental notification when protections are disabled.

Section Z. Enforcement.
App stores shall require applications to honor the child flag as a condition of listing. The Attorney General may audit compliance, require corrective action, and impose civil penalties for repeated violations.

Staff may adapt this language to fit existing statutory frameworks and enforcement authorities.

Roles & Responsibilities — Who Does What

Clear roles make enforcement practical:

  • Operating system vendors (Apple, Google): Expose Age Signaling API; document privacy model; provide developer guidance.
  • App stores: Enforce API usage as part of app review and updates.
  • App developers: Implement Child Mode API; publish youth defaults; provide parental controls and notifications.
  • State regulators / AG: Audit compliance; enforce remedies; publish enforcement actions.
  • Independent auditors / NGOs: Monitor outcomes; publish independent assessments; assist families with transparency tools.

Collaboration across these actors ensures the system is practical, transparent, and enforceable.

FAQ — Quick Answers for Families and Staff

Q: Does the Child Mode API share my child’s personal data with apps?
A: No. The API shares a single non‑identifying flag (child = true). Apps do not receive names, birthdays, contacts, photos, precise location, or browsing history.

Q: Will this break parental choice?
A: No. Parents retain control. The API reflects the parent‑verified account and apps should provide parental dashboards and re‑authorization flows for high‑risk changes.

Q: Can teens bypass this?
A: The API ties protections to the parent‑managed device/account. Teens can still access other devices, but the policy makes it harder to create a persistent, unprotected child account on the same device.

Technical Appendix · Developer Notes

Technical Appendix — API, Payload, and Implementation Notes

This appendix is intended for engineers and legislative technical staff. It explains the minimal payload, privacy model, sample SDK usage, and audit hooks.

API Payload — Minimal and Private

The Child Mode API is intentionally minimal. The OS exposes a single boolean flag to apps when a parent‑managed child account is active:

{
  "child": true,
  "version": "1.0"
}

No PII, no timestamps, no device identifiers beyond what the app already receives from the OS. Versioning allows future extensions while preserving the one‑bit safety signal.

SDK Usage — Example Pseudocode

Example pseudocode for app developers (illustrative):

// Pseudocode: check OS child flag at app startup
if (OS.getChildFlag() === true) {
  App.enableChildDefaults();
  App.disableDirectMessaging();
  App.limitDiscovery();
  App.showParentalDashboard();
} else {
  App.runStandardFlow();
}

Apps should implement idempotent logic and fail‑safe defaults: if the flag is present, protections must be active; if the flag is absent, apps continue normal behavior.

Audit Hooks & Logging

To support enforcement, apps should log events related to child defaults. Logs must be privacy‑preserving and redact PII. Suggested event types:

  • child_flag_received (timestamp, app_version)
  • child_defaults_activated (timestamp, features_enabled)
  • parental_notification_sent (timestamp, notification_type)
  • child_defaults_disabled_by_parent (timestamp, reason)

Logs should be retained for a limited period (e.g., 90 days) and made available to auditors under appropriate legal process.

Security & Abuse Considerations

Security design must prevent spoofing of the child flag. Recommendations:

  • OS vendors sign the flag with a platform key; apps verify signature.
  • Flag access requires app entitlement or permission declared in app manifest.
  • Rate limits and telemetry to detect anomalous flag requests.

These measures ensure the flag cannot be forged by malicious apps or scripts and that only authorized apps can read the flag.

Interoperability & Versioning

Versioning allows incremental rollout and backward compatibility. Example policy:

  • v1.0: boolean flag only.
  • v1.1: optional metadata for audit (non‑PII) such as platform_version and api_version.
  • v2.0+: reserved for future features after public review and privacy assessment.

Legislation should require OS vendors to publish a developer guide and a deprecation schedule for API changes.

Sample Compliance Checklist for App Stores

App stores can verify compliance during review using a short checklist:

  • Does the app read the OS child flag when present?
  • Does the app enable child defaults when the flag is present?
  • Does the app publish a youth defaults page in its privacy settings?
  • Does the app provide parental notification and re‑authorization flows?
  • Are audit logs available for review under legal process?

Failure to meet checklist items should require remediation before listing or trigger conditional listing with monitoring.
