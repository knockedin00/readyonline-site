Accountability · Apps & Platforms

Accountability in the Apps We Use

The digital world wasn’t built with kids in mind. We’re here to change that — starting with transparency, education, and a clear vision for what comes next. Our goal isn’t to promise “safe.” It’s to build safer digital lives through better design, better defaults, and better education.

Baseline Reality

Right now, every major app — Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Roblox, and Spotify — allows a child to create an adult account, even when the device is set up as a child account and parents approve downloads through Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link.

Parents assume that when they use Family Sharing or Family Link, the age of their child “translates” into the apps they approve. They expect protections to turn on automatically. Most do not realize that apps rarely receive that age information — and instead rely on whatever age the child enters manually inside the app.

This is one of the biggest knowledge gaps in digital parenting today. Parents think they’re controlling the app. Apps think nothing. Kids assume they’re safer than they are. This is the accountability gap.

Child Mode API · Age Signaling

The Child Mode API: The Baseline the Industry Has Been Missing

We believe Apple and Google should provide a simple, universal signal to apps: “This is a child account. Turn on protections automatically.” This is called Age Signaling — and it is the missing baseline for youth safety.

What Age Signaling Is

Most parents assume that when they set up Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link, and approve an app for their child, the app will automatically recognize their child’s age and activate protections. Today, that doesn’t happen.

Apps do not receive the child’s age from Family Sharing or Family Link. Instead, apps rely entirely on whatever age the child enters manually inside the app — meaning protections fail unless the child sets up their account correctly. Most parents do not realize this. Most assume the age “translates.” Most assume protections turn on automatically. They don’t.

Age Signaling is a simple, privacy‑safe way for the operating system to tell apps when a child account is in use. It does not send the child’s name, birthday, location, identity, contacts, or behavior. It sends one thing:

“Treat this user as a minor — and activate the available child protections in the app.”

Apps already read dozens of OS signals — device type, region, language, permissions, content restrictions, and screen‑time rules. Age Signaling is just one more signal, and the most important one.

What Apps Should Do When They Receive the Child Mode API

When an app receives the child flag, it should automatically:

  • Enable teen/child modes.
  • Disable risky features (open DMs, public discovery, location sharing).
  • Restrict adult, explicit, gambling, and high‑risk content.
  • Surface safety settings clearly and make them easy to understand.
  • Notify parents when protections are turned off.
  • Prevent silent activation of new features for minors.
  • Reduce reliance on self‑reported age.
  • Provide tools and guidance to help teens control what they see in their feeds and recommendations — including how to adjust algorithms per app, which many teens in our survey said they want to learn.

Apps already have teen and child modes. They simply don’t turn them on automatically. A Child Mode API connects the protections that exist with the signals parents already provide.

Privacy & Safety: Why the Child Mode API Is Safe

Parents often ask whether Age Signaling creates new privacy risks. It does not. The operating system already knows the child’s age through Family Sharing and Family Link. Parents already consent to app approvals.

Age Signaling does not send personal data to apps. Apps do not receive identity, birthday, precise location, contacts, or behavioral data. They only receive a simple safety signal: treat this user as a minor and activate protections.

This is safer than the current system, which relies on self‑reported age — the least private and least reliable method. Age Signaling is a privacy‑preserving safety signal, not a data‑sharing mechanism.

Obstacles · Accountability Gap

Why a Child Mode API Is Not a Big Ask

The biggest obstacles are not technical. They are about incentives, standards, and accountability. A Child Mode API would pull everyone together — OS vendors, app stores, apps, and families — to protect the younger generation.

Where the Obstacles Really Are

Obstacle 1 — Business incentives. Teen engagement drives revenue. Automatic child mode reduces engagement and time on platform.

Obstacle 2 — No shared standard. Every app has different defaults, settings, and risks. There is no common baseline for how minors should be treated.

Obstacle 3 — No regulatory requirement. Until states or federal law require OS‑level age signaling and app‑level enforcement, platforms are unlikely to adopt it voluntarily.

Obstacle 4 — No accountability layer. This is the most important obstacle — and the one parents need to understand.

  • OS vendors assume apps will handle youth safety.
  • Apps assume parents will handle youth safety.
  • App stores assume OS vendors will handle youth safety.
  • Parents assume OS approval handles youth safety.
  • Kids assume they’re protected.

Everyone thinks it’s someone else’s responsibility. No one owns the problem. A Child Mode API would create a shared baseline and make it clear that youth safety is a responsibility everyone shares.

Major Apps · Where They Fall Short

Where Major Apps Fall Short — and What We Expect

Each platform already knows where harm shows up — in their abuse reports, safety dashboards, fraud teams, and trust and safety investigations. We don’t want to guess. We want apps to work with us to develop honest, up‑to‑date training families can use.

Instagram (Meta)

Where this app falls short

Heavy algorithmic feeds, DMs, and discovery features are enabled by default. Teen protections rely on self‑reported age and are easy to bypass.

What we’d like to see

Honor OS‑level child signals automatically, default to teen/child modes, restrict discovery and DMs for minors, and partner with ReadyOnline to share real red‑flag patterns families need to know.

Snapchat

Where this app falls short

Ephemeral messaging, location features, and friend discovery expose teens to strangers with little visibility for parents.

What we’d like to see

Automatic child‑mode defaults for location and discovery, clearer controls for families, and collaboration on training that reflects real grooming and abuse patterns.

TikTok

Where this app falls short

Highly engaging algorithmic feeds amplify harmful content, trends, and challenges. Teen protections depend on age entry and are easy to bypass.

What we’d like to see

Automatic teen/child modes based on OS signals, stronger defaults for content limits, and partnership on training that helps families recognize manipulative trends and addictive loops — including how to adjust algorithms when feeds drift into harmful territory.

YouTube

Where this app falls short

Recommendations can surface adult content, gambling streams, and high‑risk material even when kids start from harmless videos.

What we’d like to see

Stronger child‑mode defaults tied to OS signals, clearer separation of youth and adult spaces, and training that helps families understand algorithmic drift and how to steer recommendations back to healthier content.

Roblox

Where this app falls short

User‑generated games and chat expose younger kids to older players, spending pressure, and experiences that don’t match parent expectations.

What we’d like to see

Automatic child‑mode defaults for chat, spending, and game discovery, plus training that reflects real grooming and pressure patterns Roblox already tracks in its safety systems.

Spotify

Where this app falls short

Podcasts and playlists surface adult themes and explicit content without clear age‑aware defaults. And for accounts over age 16, Spotify automatically enables in‑app chat, giving teens access to direct messaging without notifying parents.

What we’d like to see

Honor OS‑level child signals automatically, disable chat for minors by default, enforce explicit‑content restrictions consistently, and partner with ReadyOnline to help families understand how audio content and creator interactions shape beliefs and risk exposure.

Location Transparency · Authenticity

Location Transparency: A Basic Safety Signal Everyone Deserves

One of the most important digital‑literacy skills — for kids, teens, adults, and seniors — is learning how to verify who you’re interacting with online. Country‑level location for public accounts is a basic authenticity signal, not a privacy violation.

Why Country‑Level Location Matters

Many platforms do not provide even the most basic telemetry for public accounts. Meta, for example, shows country‑level location in Kid Mode for chat, but does not show country‑level origin for public posts or creators. This inconsistency removes a critical safety tool.

Country‑level location helps users:

  • Verify whether a public creator is who they claim to be.
  • Identify potential misinformation or coordinated influence campaigns.
  • Understand whether someone presenting themselves as a “local peer” is actually located elsewhere.
  • Avoid impersonation scams and social‑engineering attempts, especially for teens, adults, and seniors.

Country‑level location does not reveal private or sensitive information. It is already public for many creators on other platforms. It is a basic authenticity signal that should be available for public accounts and public figures.

If companies do not allow families to see this minimal telemetry for public accounts, they are removing one of the core skills ReadyOnline teaches: verifying identity and origin before engaging. This is essential for identifying misinformation, avoiding impersonation, and understanding the true source of content.

Advocacy · State & Federal

Turning Accountability into Action

We believe the Child Mode API should be required by state and federal law. Connecticut is a perfect place to start — and we intend to advocate for legislation that makes OS‑level age signaling and app‑level enforcement a legal baseline.

Our Legislative Position

We will advocate for laws that require:

  • OS‑level Age Signaling for child accounts.
  • App stores to pass age signals through to apps.
  • Apps to honor the Child Mode API and activate protections automatically.
  • Transparent documentation of how apps treat minors by default.
  • Consistent child‑mode defaults for communication, discovery, content, and spending.
  • Country‑level location visibility for public accounts and creators, as a basic authenticity signal.

This is realistic, achievable, and overdue. It does not require new categories of personal data. It simply connects the age information parents already provide to the protections apps already have.
