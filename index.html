Accountability in the Apps We Use

The digital world wasn’t built with kids in mind. We’re here to change that — starting with transparency, education, and a clear vision for what comes next. Our goal isn’t to promise “safe.” It’s to build safer digital lives through better design, better defaults, and better education.

There Is No Single Solution

Even if every device is set up as a child account, every app honors age signals, and every parent configures settings perfectly, kids still need awareness, reflexes, judgment, and support. Technology alone cannot teach digital instincts. That’s why ReadyOnline focuses on both monitoring and education — together they work better than either one alone.

Where We Are Today

Right now, every major app — Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, Discord, Spotify, Roblox, YouTube and more — allows a child to create an adult account, even when:

  • The device is set up as a child account
  • The operating system knows the child’s real age
  • The parent approved the download through Apple Family Sharing or Android Family Link

Apps rely on self‑reported age, which kids can bypass in seconds. Parents assume protections are in place. Kids assume they’re safer than they are. Apps assume nothing. This is the accountability gap.

Why Child Accounts Alone Aren’t Enough

Locking everything down without explanation doesn’t teach kids how to recognize manipulation, pressure, scams, or risky situations. It doesn’t build the reflex to pause, ask questions, or reach out for help early. Child accounts are a starting point, not a complete solution. Kids need both guardrails and education to navigate digital life with confidence.

The Child Mode API: Step One, Not the Finish Line

We believe operating systems should provide a simple, universal signal to apps: “This is a child account. Turn on protections automatically.” This is the Child Mode API — a basic capability that should be included in Apple and Android SDKs and honored by every major app.

What the Child Mode API Should Do

When an app receives the child‑account signal, it should:
  • Automatically enable teen/child modes and safer defaults
  • Disable or restrict risky features by default (open DMs, public discovery, location sharing)
  • Limit exposure to adult, gambling, and high‑risk content categories
  • Make safety settings visible and understandable to both teens and parents
  • Notify parents when protections are turned off or risky features are enabled
This doesn’t replace education — it makes it easier for families to use the protections apps already claim to provide.

Apps Must Be Held Accountable

Today, most platforms claim to “self‑monitor” youth safety. In practice, they rely on self‑reported age, bury safety settings, and enable risky features by default. That’s not real protection. The Accountability page exists to call out where major apps fall short and to invite them to do better — not just by giving parents tools, but by partnering to create honest, up‑to‑date training based on the risks they already see in their own reporting.

Where Major Apps Fall Short — and What We Expect

Instagram (Meta)
Where this app falls short

Heavy algorithmic feeds, DMs, and discovery features are enabled by default. Teen protections are inconsistent and rely on self‑reported age.

What We’d Like to See

Honor OS‑level child signals automatically, default to teen/child modes, restrict discovery and DMs for minors, and partner with ReadyOnline to share real red‑flag patterns so families can learn how harm actually shows up on the platform.

Snapchat
Where this app falls short

Ephemeral messaging, location features, and friend discovery can expose teens to strangers and risky interactions with very little visibility for parents.

What We’d Like to See

Automatic child‑mode defaults for location and discovery, clearer controls for parents and teens, and collaboration on training that reflects real abuse and grooming patterns seen in Snapchat’s own safety reports.

TikTok
Where this app falls short

Highly engaging, algorithm‑driven feeds can amplify harmful content, trends, and challenges. Teen protections depend on age entry and are easy to bypass.

What We’d Like to See

Automatic teen/child modes based on OS signals, stronger defaults for content limits, and partnership with ReadyOnline to teach families how to recognize manipulative trends, harmful challenges, and addictive loops.

Discord
Where this app falls short

Open servers, private channels, and invite links can expose teens to unmoderated spaces, gambling picks, and adult communities with minimal friction.

What We’d Like to See

Child‑mode defaults that restrict server discovery, clearer controls for parents, and collaboration on training around red flags in gaming, gambling, and high‑risk communities that Discord already tracks internally.

YouTube
Where this app falls short

Recommendations can surface adult content, gambling streams, and high‑risk material even when kids start from seemingly harmless videos.

What We’d Like to See

Stronger child‑mode defaults tied to OS signals, clearer separation of youth and adult spaces, and partnership on training that helps families understand how recommendation systems can drift into harmful territory.

Roblox
Where this app falls short

User‑generated games and chat can expose younger kids to older players, in‑game spending pressure, and experiences that don’t match parent expectations.

What We’d Like to See

Automatic child‑mode defaults for chat, spending, and game discovery, plus collaboration on training that reflects real grooming, pressure, and spending patterns Roblox already sees in its trust and safety data.

Spotify
Where this app falls short

Podcasts and playlists can surface adult themes, gambling content, and explicit material without clear age‑aware defaults for younger listeners.

What We’d Like to See

Child‑aware defaults for podcasts and explicit content, clearer controls for families, and partnership on training that helps parents understand how audio content can shape beliefs, habits, and risk exposure over time.

We Don’t Want to Guess — We Want to Partner

Each of these platforms already knows where harm shows up: in their abuse reports, safety dashboards, fraud teams, and trust and safety investigations. ReadyOnline doesn’t want to guess at those patterns. We want apps to work with us to develop honest, up‑to‑date training that families can use — based on the real red flags platforms see every day.

Beyond Apps: Banks and Insurance Companies

Financial institutions and insurance companies already track scams, fraud, identity theft, and social engineering at scale. Their fraud and risk teams see how teens and young adults are targeted — and how families are impacted. Our long‑term vision is to partner with these teams to build red‑flag training that helps families recognize financial manipulation early, before harm escalates.

Monitoring + Education + Accountability

ReadyOnline is built on a simple idea: monitoring and education together work better than either one alone. Parents cannot carry this burden by themselves. Apps must do their part. OS vendors must expose child‑account signals in their SDKs. Banks and insurers must share what they know about fraud and scams. And kids must learn the reflexes that keep them safer online.

Kids deserve a digital world built with them in mind. ReadyOnline is here to help families navigate the world we have today — and to push the industry toward the world we expect tomorrow.
